Heat pump or gas water heater: which is cheaper to run where you live?

Updated 2026-11-05 · Next refresh 2026-12-05 · Electricity prices 2026-08 · Gas prices 2025 annual

The answer

On operating cost alone, a heat pump water heater is cheaper to run than an efficient gas storage heater in 28 of the 45 places with both prices on file (44 states and the District of Columbia), and gas is cheaper in 17. 8 are close calls, within $10 a year either way. Prices are EIA averages: electricity 2026-08, natural gas 2025 annual, retrieved 2026-11-05. At UEF 3.5 the heat pump wins in 28 places, at UEF 2.9 in 19. At the U.S. average prices (18.2 cents per kWh, $15.34 per thousand cubic feet) the two cost about $121 and $146 a year.

How we calculated this

We assume hot water needs 2,340 kWh of delivered heat a year, about 39 gallons a day; DOE's medium test pattern is 55 gallons a day, so if your household uses more, set the hot-water-use box to high (1.4 times). A heat pump water heater turns that into heat at a UEF of 3.5, an efficient gas storage heater at 0.81 and an electric resistance tank at 0.93; gas is priced at 10.37 therms per thousand cubic feet. Prices are the EIA state residential averages: electricity for 2026-08 (one month, which can sit above the yearly average in summer) and natural gas for the 2025 annual average, The EIA residential gas price is total revenue divided by gas delivered, so it includes the fixed monthly customer charge spread over the gas used. The EIA electricity price is the same kind of all-in average (revenue divided by kWh sold). For a home that keeps gas for heating or cooking anyway, the extra gas a water heater burns costs less than the average, so the gas cost here is overstated, more so than electricity because a fixed charge is a bigger share of a small gas bill; that favours the heat pump in the table. EIA marks its 2026 electricity values preliminary, and 2026 gas prices have moved since the 2025 average. A heat pump rated at UEF 3.5 often delivers less in practice, which favours gas; at UEF 2.9 the heat pump is cheaper in 19 of the 45 places and at 2.5 in 13. Try the efficiency box. Missing from the table because EIA lists no 2025 annual gas price (NA) for them: Connecticut, Maine, Maryland, North Carolina, Rhode Island, South Carolina. The gas heater is modelled at UEF 0.81, a condensing-class efficiency; a standard tank (about 0.64) costs more to run, which favours the heat pump further. These are operating costs only: install cost, rebates (the federal 25C tax credit ended for water heaters installed after December 31, 2025, per the IRS; check your utility and state for current rebates), and the way a heat pump cools and slows in a cold basement are not included. For scale: a US Department of Energy Building America field study of Northeast installs found confined-space installations lost about 16% of efficiency; entering 2.9 in the efficiency box approximates a 16% loss from 3.5 (DOE case study). We did not test any water heater. See how we rank.
