How this ranking is scored

We filtered every ENERGY STAR certified residential clothes dryer to those whose certification record flags heat pump technology (hybrid heat pump models excluded), kept the full-size ventless models only (compact dryers are certified on a smaller test load, so their yearly kWh is not comparable), merged identical models sold under several brand names, and ranked on certified Combined Energy Factor (CEF, 95% of the score); the shorter DOE test cycle makes up the other 5%, so dryers with the same CEF are ordered by cycle length. Yearly kWh is ENERGY STAR's own estimate for DOE's 283 test loads; we price it at the EIA U.S. average residential rate and multiply by five. We did not test any dryer. A dryer that dries faster in the DOE test can still take longer in your home: the test uses a fixed load and moisture target. The Midea MLE27N5AWWC certifies a CEF of 10.14 as a standard ventless dryer but its record carries no heat pump flag; we follow the record and leave it out. The LG WashTower stacked dryer is also certified but is left out of the ranking because LG lists it as discontinued. The Beko HPD24414W and the Blomberg DHP24404W3 listed near the top appear to be the same Arcelik machine in an earlier and a later revision (this page's sold-as record lists the later number as also sold as the Beko model); ENERGY STAR certifies them separately and their certified figures differ only in test cycle length, so their order here rests on that cycle difference alone and the two are effectively one choice. The LG DLHC5502 and DLHC6702 are certified heat pump dryers that are not ranked because we could not confirm their seller page.

  • Combined Energy Factor (95%): pounds of dry clothes per kWh in the U.S. DOE test; higher is better. It is 95% of the score; the test cycle time (shorter is better) is the other 5% (higher is better)
  • Certified yearly energy (0%): shown, not scored: ENERGY STAR's estimate for DOE's 283 loads a year on the full-size test load; it follows from the Combined Energy Factor, so it mostly repeats that ranking in kWh (lower is better)
  • 5-year electricity cost (0%): shown, not scored (it follows from the certified yearly energy): the yearly energy priced at the current U.S. average residential rate (EIA); your state's rate changes it (lower is better) Computed: certified yearly kWh x EIA U.S. average residential price x 5.
  • Test cycle time (5%): scored at 5% as a tiebreak so equal-efficiency dryers are ordered by something real: length of the DOE test cycle, the nearest certified figure for the common worry that heat pump dryers are slow; shorter is better (lower is better)
  • Drum (0%): shown, not scored: a bigger drum dries more per load; all models here are full-size (higher is better)

Each number is scored against the best on this page (best = 100%; e.g. 90% of the best capacity = 0.9) and multiplied by its weight; the total is out of 100. Full method.

FAQ

Are heat pump dryers more efficient than vented dryers?

On the certified numbers, yes by a wide margin: the full-size heat pump models here reach a CEF of 9.10 to 11.00 lb/kWh. Vented dryers certify a median CEF of 3.93, and ventless dryers without a heat pump typically do worse (median 2.68), which is why this page filters on the heat pump flag in the certification record.

How much does a heat pump dryer cost to run?

The certified yearly energy of the models here is 217 to 263 kWh, which is 197 to 239 US dollars over five years at 0.1816 dollars per kWh (EIA, 2026-08). Your rate and how many loads you dry change this. For comparison, certified vented dryers use a median of about 608 kWh a year, so a heat pump dryer saves roughly 340 to 390 kWh, about 60 to 70 US dollars a year at the same price, before your own loads and rate.

Do heat pump dryers take longer?

In the DOE test cycle, these models run 53 to 80 minutes. That is the certified test cycle on a fixed load, not a promise for your clothes.

Where do these numbers come from?

From the U.S. EPA ENERGY STAR certified Clothes Dryers dataset (retrieved 2026-11-05) and the U.S. EIA Electric Power Monthly (2026-08). We rank the certified results and compute the running cost; we did not run our own tests.
