How this ranking is scored

We kept every battery-electric model of model year 2025 or later in the EPA and DOE fueleconomy.gov data, listed each model once in its most efficient EPA-rated configuration of any model year in the data, and ranked on the EPA combined kWh per 100 miles. Because the EPA rates every wheel and trim configuration separately, the spread to the model's least efficient configuration is shown. Cost assumes 1,000 miles a month (our assumption) at the EIA U.S. average residential price, charged at home. Efficiency and range are EPA label figures, not our tests; real use varies with temperature, speed and load, and size and performance change the figure. We did not test any vehicle. The list mixes body styles and sizes, and a large SUV and a small sedan differ in efficiency mostly because of size, so compare within a class rather than across the whole list. A model that appears only as a model-year-2025 row, such as the Hyundai Ioniq 6 Standard Range, may no longer be sold new.

  • Energy use (95%): EPA combined-cycle electricity per 100 miles on the fuel economy label (lower is better); the whole page ranks on this one EPA figure (lower is better)
  • Rated range (5%): EPA label range on a full charge; scored lightly (5 %) so models with the same electricity use are told apart by the one who goes farther; a bigger battery adds range and weight (higher is better)
  • Home charging cost per month (0%): shown, not scored (it follows from the EPA figure): 1,000 miles a month is our assumption, priced at the current U.S. average residential rate (EIA); home charging only (lower is better) Computed: EPA kWh per 100 miles x 10 (1,000 miles a month) x EIA U.S. average residential price.
  • Spread across this model's configurations (0%): shown, not scored: how many more kWh per 100 miles this model's least efficient configuration (larger wheels, more motors) uses than the one listed (lower is better)
  • Variants merged into this row (0%): shown, not scored: how many EPA-rated configurations of this model (same drive type, model years 2025 and later) exist (lower is better)

Each number is scored against the best on this page (best = 100%; e.g. 90% of the best capacity = 0.9) and multiplied by its weight; the total is out of 100. Full method.

FAQ

How much does choosing an efficient EV save?

The median electric model of this period uses about 32.5 kWh per 100 miles in the EPA data; the models here use 23.1 to 27.0. At 1,000 miles a month and 0.1816 dollars per kWh (EIA, 2026-08) the most efficient one costs about 200 US dollars a year less to charge at home than the median model, though sizes differ: compare like with like (a small sedan against a small sedan), and see the Variants column for how much wheel and trim choices move the figure.

Which electric car is the most efficient?

On the EPA label figures, the Lucid Air Pure RWD with 19 inch wheels (2027) at 23.1 kWh/100 mi; the models listed here run from 23.1 to 27.0 kWh per 100 miles. Size and performance drive the figure, so compare like with like.

How much does it cost to charge an electric car at home?

At 1,000 miles a month and 0.1816 dollars per kWh (EIA, 2026-08), the most efficient models on this page cost 42 to 49 US dollars a month in home electricity. Across all 91 models the EPA figure runs from 23.1 to 63.9 kWh per 100 miles, from efficient sedans to large SUVs, pickups and performance cars. Your miles and your rate change this.

Where do these numbers come from?

From the EPA and DOE fueleconomy.gov Vehicle Fuel Economy Data (retrieved 2026-11-05) and the U.S. EIA Electric Power Monthly (2026-08). We rank the published EPA results and compute the running cost; we did not run our own tests.
